The king of Atri hung a new bell in the tower of the market place, and the whole town came down the hill to look at it. It was polished until it shone as yellow as the sun. Its rope hung almost to the ground, so that the smallest child could ring it.

"How we should like to hear it ring!" the people said. Then the king came down the street. "Perhaps he will ring it," they whispered, and everybody stood very still.

But he did not ring the bell. He did not even take the rope in his hands. He stopped at the foot of the tower and raised his hand.

"My people," he said, "do you see this beautiful bell? It is your bell, but it must never be rung except in case of need. If any one of you is wronged, he may come and ring it, and the judges shall come together at once and hear his case and give him justice. Rich and poor, old and young, all alike may come. But no one must touch the rope unless he knows that he has been wronged. This is the bell of justice."

Years went by. Many times the bell rang out to call the judges together, and many wrongs were put right. At last the rope was almost worn out. Its end came untwisted and broke away, strand by strand, until only a tall man could reach it.

"This will never do," said the judges. "What if a child should be wronged? It could not ring the bell to let us know."

There was not a rope to be had in all Atri, and one from across the mountains would take many days.

"Let me fix it for you," said a man who stood by. He ran into his garden and came back with a long grapevine, climbed up, and tied it to the bell. The slender vine trailed to the ground with its leaves and tendrils still on it.

"Yes," said the judges, "it is a very good rope. Let it be as it is."

On the hillside above the town lived a man who had once been a brave knight. In his youth he had ridden through many lands and fought in many battles, and his best friend through all that time had been his horse, who had carried him safely through many dangers.

But when the knight grew older he cared no more to ride into battle; he cared no more to do brave deeds; he thought of nothing but gold, and he became a miser. He sold everything he had except his horse. Day after day he sat among his money bags, planning how to get more gold. Day after day his horse stood in a bare stall, half-starved and shivering with cold.

"Why should I keep that lazy beast?" the miser said to himself one morning. "Every week he costs me more than he is worth. I might sell him, but there is not a man who wants him. I cannot even give him away. I will turn him out to find what he can. If he starves, so much the better."

So the brave old horse was turned out on the stony hillside. Lame and sick, he wandered the dusty roads, glad to find a blade of grass or a thistle. Boys threw stones at him, dogs barked at him, and in all the world there was no one to pity him.

One hot afternoon he wandered into the market place. Not a man or a child was there, for the heat of the sun had driven them all indoors. The gates stood wide open, and the poor beast could go where he pleased. He saw the grapevine hanging from the bell, its leaves still fresh and green, for it had not been there long. What a fine dinner they would be for a starving horse!

He stretched out his thin neck and took a leaf in his teeth. It was hard to break it from the vine. He pulled, and the great bell above him began to ring. All Atri heard it, and it seemed to say:

"Someone has done me wrong! Someone has done me wrong! Oh, come and judge my case! Oh, come and judge my case! For I've been wronged!"

The judges heard it. They put on their robes and hurried through the hot streets, wondering who could be ringing at such an hour. When they came through the gate, there was no one at the rope but the old horse, nibbling at the vine.

"Ha!" said one. "It is the miser's horse. He has come to call for justice, for his master, as everybody knows, has treated him shamefully."

"He pleads his case as well as a horse can," said another.

"And he shall have justice!" said the third.

By now men and women and children had crowded into the market place. When they saw the horse, they all stood still in wonder. Then every one of them had something to tell: how they had seen him wandering on the hills, unfed and uncared for, while his master sat at home counting his gold.

"Go and bring the miser before us," said the judges.

When he came, they made him stand and hear their judgment. "This horse has served you well for many a year," they said. "He has saved you from many a danger. He has helped you win your gold. So we order that half of all your gold shall be set aside to buy him shelter and food, a green field to graze in, and a warm stall to comfort him in his old age."

The miser hung his head and grieved to lose his gold. But the people shouted with joy, and the old horse was led away to his new stall and a dinner such as he had not had in many a day.
