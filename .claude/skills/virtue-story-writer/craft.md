# Craft

Techniques drawn from narrative nonfiction practice (Jack Hart's *Storycraft*;
Tracy Kidder and Richard Todd, who met at The Atlantic, in *Good Prose*; Mark
Kramer on scene; Michael Pollan on endings), from Bennett's anthology, and from
read-aloud editing. These are tools, not a checklist: use the ones the source
calls for. Every example is written for this file, each from a different tale, so
no single story's shape becomes the model. Examples come only from published
stories or tales kept off `candidates.md`, so no candidate arrives half-written.

## 1. Open on the first beat where something can go wrong

Name the actor in the first or second sentence and let them do something. By the
end of paragraph one the listener should feel what could go wrong. Setup that the
source tells before the trouble (a founding, a backstory, a proclamation) may be
folded into clauses inside that first scene; source order governs what happens on
stage, not how much preamble comes first.

If the source carries an object through the tale (a cup, a cloak, a rope), let the
reader see it early. Do not plant one the source lacks so that the ending can
return to it.

> Before: Atri is a little town in Italy, built halfway up a hill, and long ago its
> king hung a bell in the market place.
>
> After: The rope on the bell of justice had worn so short that only a tall man
> could reach it, and the judges of Atri began to worry about the children.

## 2. The telling detail

One concrete detail per scene that only this story could contain and that moves
the story or shows what is at stake. Prefer the source's own details; old texts
are full of them. Test: could the detail sit in any other story? Then replace it.

> Before: The wind blew very hard, but the traveler would not take off his cloak.
>
> After: The harder the wind blew, the tighter the traveler pulled his cloak, until
> he was walking bent double with both fists at his throat.

Aesop's North Wind loses because of one detail, the fists at the throat: the
harder you push, the harder he holds.

## 3. Slow down at the decisive act

Summarize travel and time; dramatize the act. Move in real time: what is seen and
done, in the order it happens, and the small physical thing that decides it. The
act may belong to one chooser, to a court, or to a creature that cannot speak.
When the act is a habit kept (a prayer at the usual window), slow down on the
habit itself, not on a choice the source never shows; never invent hesitation or
cost the source lacks. Give the decisive act at least as many sentences as the
longest scene around it.

> Before: The honest woodcutter refused the golden axe, showing his true and humble
> heart.
>
> After: Mercury held up an axe of gold. The woodcutter looked at it a long while,
> at the bright edge and the handle no woodsman would use. "That is not mine," he
> said.

The looking is in the fable; the length of the look is the scene. Nothing is
added that the woodcutter does not do.

## 4. The turn

Each story has a beat where what a character knows or does changes. Mark it with
an event, never an announcement ("something strange happened"), and do not spend
it early: "one last time," "for the final time," and "little did he know" give the
outcome away. The turn needs no special formatting. A one-sentence paragraph is
the strongest pause a read-aloud text has; use one only where the source itself
turns, and not in every story.

## 5. Dialogue

Few lines, each one moving the act. Use "said" and "asked," or no tag when the
speaker is clear; an action beat instead of an adverb. Characters speak plainly in
their station and era. No invented speeches: past two sentences, cut or hand part
of it to a gesture. An attested speech (a decree, a verdict) may run longer; tell
its procedural part as reported speech and keep its attested refrain in quotation
marks. Keep dialogue under about a third of the story (the linter warns past 40
percent). Keep every line the source attests, chants included (see §10).

> Before: "You must learn to be content with less!" his mother exclaimed wisely.
>
> After: His mother knelt down by the jar. "Take half," she said, "and your hand will
> come out."

## 6. Read-aloud breath

A tired parent reads this aloud with a child leaning on one arm. Checks you can
actually run:

- In every sentence over 20 words, the main verb arrives within the first eight
  words and the stress word comes last. Long sentences built as a chain of actions
  joined by "and" read well aloud; clauses stacked before the verb do not.
- Each pronoun's owner is in the same or the previous sentence.
- No word spelled alike but said differently (read, lead, tear, wound, bow),
  except inside an attested refrain ("live for ever"), where context fixes it.
- No sentence opens with a participle ("Trembling, he...").
- `writing.md` allows parentheses; aloud they cannot be voiced, so prefer a comma
  or a new sentence.
- Vary sentence length on purpose, and vary it differently from story to story.

## 7. Restraint

Emotion travels by gesture and object, not adjective. Kidder and Todd call the
honest writer a restrained illusionist. Keep the one modifier that helps a child
see; cut the rest. Give a telling detail once and let the character act on it,
instead of describing it, explaining it, and stating what it proves.

> Before: Filled with compassion, the kind ant felt sorry for the shivering
> grasshopper.
>
> After: The ant looked at the grasshopper's thin legs. Then she went inside and
> came back with a handful of grain.

## 8. End on the source's strongest beat

Pollan's test for an ending: it signals the end, lands the point, and echoes. End
on the source's own last strong beat at full strength: an action, a line of
speech, a plain consequence, or an image. A child is owed the payoff the source
gives (the horse's dinner, the dry ground); never trade it for a quiet image of
your own. Then stop: cut any sentence after it, especially one that begins "From
that day," "And so," or "He had learned." The explicit reflection waits for
`virtueDescription`.

Endings vary with their sources. A feast, a verdict, a line of dialogue, a door
opening, a laugh: let the tale decide. A callback to the opening is one option
among many, and only when the source supplies it.

## 9. Depth: worth an adult's attention

Bennett chose stories a child understands and an adult keeps thinking about. Keep
the complication the source provides: the cost not repaid, the character who is
not purely good, the irony a child will grow into (a whole town had seen the
starving horse and done nothing until a bell made it a case). Do not resolve
what the source leaves open.

## 10. Retelling, not inventing

The house retells; it does not invent. The brief names one **governing text**, the
edition the draft is faithful to event for event.

- Keep its events, their order on stage, the characters and their names, and any
  line it puts in a speaker's mouth, lightly modernized at most. Replace a word
  whose meaning has shifted for a child ("dumb brute," "gay," "intercourse") with
  the plain sense and record the change in the gate note.
- Keep its refrains, chants, counted repetitions, and any line a child would say
  along with the reader ("Some one has done me wrong!"). They are the read-aloud
  payload: cut setup before you cut them, and ask for a length exception before
  you cut them for length.
- Keep its best phrases where they still read aloud cleanly ("no rest for the sole
  of her foot"). Paraphrasing a great line into a plain one is a loss, not a
  modernization.
- You may add sensory detail consistent with time and place, interior beats the
  action implies, and compression of repeated episodes.
- You may not add events, named characters, miracles, motives that contradict the
  source, or words for real historical people beyond what a source records.
- Scripture: tell what the passage says at the scale it says it, no added sermon.
  The brief sets the passage (provenance.md step 5); do not reach past it to
  soften or extend, and do not soften what happens inside it.
- Fables: the collector's closing moral is a later tag; it becomes
  `virtueDescription` material, never body text.
- Legends attached to real people are told as tales; `source` says whose.

## 11. Beat the source

A retelling earns its place only if a parent would rather read it aloud than the
governing text. Before drafting, name what yours will do better for a child at
bedtime (plainer breath, a scene where the source summarizes, a clearer turn) and
what it must keep because the source already does it best. If the honest answer
is "nothing," the source should be reprinted, not retold. The editor tests this
blind.

## 12. Avoid house habits

One house voice is the point of a retold anthology: the same restraint, the same
care with old texts. Formula is its failure: twenty stories that repeat the same
quotable moves. A blind reader of three house stories side by side heard these;
vary them across the catalog:

- the first sentence: not always a subject followed by past-perfect backstory
  ("had watched," "had drifted");
- the last sentence: not always one long "and" chain closing on the source's last
  clause, and not always the same gesture (looking out, waiting, light, rest
  restored). Endings differ the way their sources do: a verdict, a feast, a line of
  speech, a short plain fact;
- paragraph openers: "So" and "Then" are the scripture register's, not yours; no
  more than two paragraphs per story open on either;
- echoes: a phrase planted early and returned at the close is a device the second
  time a reader meets it. Use one only when the source itself makes the echo
  (Daniel's "live for ever"), never as an implied beat of your own;
- archaisms kept as souvenirs: keep the source's refrain in its own words, not a
  sprinkle of "exceedingly," "behold," and "lo" around it;
- refrains of your own: a doubled negative ("She did not come back. She did not
  come back...") is strong once in the catalog and a tic the second time;
- sentence rhythm: a scripture retelling, a fable, and a schoolroom legend should
  not share one spread of sentence lengths.

The linter measures these against every draft and the live catalog
(`house-tic`, `invented-echo`, `pet-openers`, `archaism-souvenirs`), and the
editor runs a blind "house voice or formula?" read when a draft joins the catalog,
which fails a draft only on quotable shared tics.

## 13. Bennett's frame lives outside the body

Bennett introduced each selection with a short note. Here the `summary` sets up
the tale for a browsing parent and `virtueDescription` gives the one reflection
after it. The body never introduces, frames, or sums up.
